The affordability gap What to fund Health evidence How to measure progress

In 2025, an estimated 645 million people habitually consumed too little dietary energy. About 2.69 billion people could not afford even the least expensive healthy diet available to them after accounting for basic nonfood needs. Those figures describe different problems, and the populations overlap. A household can get enough calories while relying on a narrow range of inexpensive foods. Both problems can persist even when a country produces more food. The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2026 · FAO release

The way forward is to bring the cost of nutritious food within reach and protect people’s access when incomes, crops or supply routes fail. That requires two decisions at once. Give households enough money to buy a healthy diet where markets work. Make that diet cheaper, closer and easier to prepare. Where markets or safe access break down, deliver food and nutrition care through emergency channels.

Put a number on the gap families face

Start with the local cost of a healthy basket and the amount a household can spend after essentials such as housing and transport. Imagine a family has 90 units of its local currency left for food each week, and the basket costs 120. Getting the price down to 110 and adding 20 in usable grocery benefits would close that 30-unit gap. That is an illustration. An actual benefit level requires local prices, household size and income data, plus a plan for seasons when food gets more expensive.

A basket also needs to work in real life. Families need a reachable seller, safe water, enough time and fuel to cook, and foods they can use and want to eat. Healthy diets can be built from many combinations of vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and other appropriate nutrient sources. Children and adults have different needs. Use local prices and food traditions to design the basket with the people who would buy it. WHO healthy diet guidance · World Bank Food Prices for Nutrition

Four changes to fund

  1. Close the income gap and keep assistance available in a crisis. Use predictable cash or grocery benefits where markets can reliably restock, with offline options for people who lack digital access. Track which eligible households miss a payment and whether the benefit keeps up with local food prices. Agree in advance who can release emergency funds when a drought, flood or price spike threatens access. Where stores are empty or reaching them is dangerous, fund safe humanitarian access, food delivery and nutrition care. In a Bangladesh flood trial, giving the same cash shortly before the flood peak reduced food insecurity in the first weeks compared with paying later. The advantage largely faded once all groups had been paid. World Bank social protection report · WFP and World Bank flood trial

  2. Lower the retail price of nutritious food. FAO’s 2026 analysis finds that fruits, vegetables and animal-source foods account for close to 70% of the modeled cost of a healthy diet. Ask farmers, traders and shoppers where costs rise. In one district the answer may be poor roads or unusable storage. In another it may be seasonal production gaps, expensive energy or few affordable retailers. Invest accordingly in locally adapted crops and extension, dependable transport, storage, or markets that carry affordable fresh, frozen, dried or canned foods. Test whether retail prices come down for households with the least money and whether farmers can still earn enough to supply the market. SOFI 2026, chapters 3–5

  3. Use schools, stores and clinics that people already reach. Provide nutritious school meals, protect access during holidays, and connect pregnant people, infants and young children to nutrition services. In the U.S. Summer EBT randomized demonstration, $60 per eligible school-age child per month reduced very low food security among children from 9.1% to 6.1% relative to no summer benefit. Massachusetts’ randomized 30% produce rebate increased adults’ targeted fruit and vegetable intake by 0.24 cup-equivalents per day (95% confidence interval 0.13–0.34). These are measured gains in access or intake. When a benefit is hard to redeem, expand participating retailers and check actual use before scaling it. USDA Summer EBT evaluation, Exhibits 4.5–4.6 · Produce rebate trial · WHO and UNICEF wasting guidance

  4. Make the healthier option the easier option. Offer free safe drinking water and use nutrition standards for publicly funded meals. Where governments use taxes on sugary drinks, measure which products people switch to and what low-income households pay. Seattle’s 2026 budget directs some beverage-tax revenue toward produce benefits and healthy food in schools. A separate evaluation across five U.S. cities found sugary-drink purchase volume fell after taxes, with a wide range around the estimate. Purchases are an early signal. Test whether a local policy improves overall diets and whether the benefits reach the households carrying the cost. Seattle 2026 adopted budget · Five-city purchase evaluation

What better diets could change for health

Heart disease and stroke. The DASH randomized feeding trial lowered systolic blood pressure 5.5 mm Hg more than the control diet. In the corrected PREDIMED analysis, major cardiovascular events occurred in 3.8% of high-risk adults assigned a Mediterranean-style diet with extra-virgin olive oil and 3.4% with nuts, versus 4.4% with lower-fat advice over a median 4.8 years. The study supplied foods and support, and the corrected analysis addressed departures from random assignment. These findings show what sustained diet changes can achieve in defined populations. DASH trial · Corrected PREDIMED study

Obesity and type 2 diabetes. Better access makes it easier to eat well consistently. Obesity also has biological, social and environmental causes. In a small, eight-week randomized feeding comparison, adults who received a minimally processed diet lost about one percentage point more body weight than those who received an ultra-processed diet. Both menus followed UK dietary guidance. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, intensive diet, activity and weight-management support lowered diabetes incidence in high-risk adults from 11.0 to 4.8 cases per 100 person-years compared with placebo. Both interventions provided more than a lower food price. In one 2026 trial, home-delivered groceries with recipes and an offer of counseling improved a marker of blood sugar control. In another, a monthly produce subsidy offered to 2,155 adults produced no improvement. The trials enrolled different people and tested different programs. Measure how people use each benefit and what changes for their health. WHO obesity overview · Feeding trial · Diabetes Prevention Program · 2026 grocery delivery trial · 2026 produce subsidy trial

Depression. Diet support may help some people alongside depression care. A small 12-week trial found remission in 10 of 31 people assessed after dietary support versus 2 of 25 after social support. A much larger prevention trial did not find that its tested interventions prevented new cases of major depression. The effect of affordable healthy food on depression at population scale remains uncertain. SMILES trial · MooDFOOD prevention trial

Child growth and wasting. Children need enough energy, a varied diet and timely care when sick. In one northern Bangladesh trial, cash plus nutrition coaching improved children’s dietary diversity and reduced stunting by 7.8 percentage points versus the control group (approximate 95% confidence interval 1.9–13.7 points lower). A separate intervention in the south improved dietary diversity without a detectable effect on growth. Preventing and treating wasting also requires early detection and health services that families can reach. Bangladesh trial, Tables 2, 3 and 7 · WHO and UNICEF wasting guidance

How to know it’s working

In the first 90 days, have finance, health, agriculture and social protection teams work with communities to map who cannot afford the local basket and why. Fund one change that closes the largest verified gap, plus an emergency plan with a named decision-maker, a funding trigger and a way to reach people when the usual system fails. Emergency help should continue during planning.

Within a year, publish what changed in food prices, missed meals, diet diversity, benefit take-up and missed deliveries. Show results by district and for groups routinely missed. Track validated nutrition and health outcomes separately, over enough time to interpret them. Compare against current practice where feasible. Show what the program cost to deliver, including staff time, storage, transport and maintenance. Use the results to expand what people can actually use and fix what shuts them out.

The World Bank estimates that scaling a defined package of undernutrition interventions would require $13 billion in additional funding annually over 2025–2034. Ending hunger also takes money for purchasing power, emergency relief and the food system that gets affordable nutritious foods to market. Each country needs a budget that reflects its own barriers and puts recurring costs on the books. World Bank investment framework

The first test of success is whether fewer families skip meals and more can afford and eat a varied diet all year. If those gains last, the evidence gives us good reason to expect better health. The size of the benefit will depend on who is reached, how diets change, and whether people can keep eating well through the next shock.
